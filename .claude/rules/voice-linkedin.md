## Machine-writing tells to avoid

These are craft rules, not claims about Alif. They hold regardless of the interview.

- The three-part escalating list where the third item is abstract. "Faster, cleaner, more human." Delete.
- "It's not X. It's Y." Once in a post is a move. Twice is a template.
- Rhetorical question as an opener. "Ever wondered why...?"
- "Here's the thing." "The truth is." "Let that sink in."
- Single-sentence paragraphs stacked for false drama, six in a row.
- Starting sentences with "And" or "But" more than twice in one post to fake conversational rhythm.
- Summarising the post in the last line. Real writing ends; it does not recap.
- Any sentence that would survive unchanged in someone else's post. If it is portable, it is not voice.
- Adjective pairs: "simple yet powerful", "small but meaningful".
- Numbers that arrive suspiciously round. "We cut research time by 40%."
- The word "journey" used about anything other than a literal user journey.

## Usual post shape

`[G — this whole section is a hypothesis]`

1. Two lines that are a specific moment, not a thesis. A room, a date, a thing said out loud.
2. The situation in three or four short paragraphs. What was actually happening. Who wanted what.
3. The turn. The thing he noticed that the room did not.
4. What he now does differently, stated plainly and without generalising it into a rule for everyone.
5. An ending that stops. A genuine question or a flat observation. Never a call to action.

Length: 150 to 300 words. `[S — stated in the existing writer prompt]`

## Formatting

- Line breaks between paragraphs, always. LinkedIn's mobile rendering makes a wall of text unreadable.
- Paragraphs of one to three sentences. Not one sentence each, which reads as performance.
- No bold, no headers. LinkedIn does not render markdown and the raw asterisks look careless.
- Bullets only when the content is genuinely a list of parallel things, which is rarely.
- No hashtags in the body. If any, one or two at the very bottom, and honestly they do very little now. `[G]`

## Named post formats

Seven. Use these names in the Notion queue's Format column, and say in one line why the chosen one fits.

**1. The Room.** A specific meeting, review or workshop. Someone said something out loud. The post is carried by that quote and what it revealed. Fails without a real quote. Never name the person.

**2. The Correction.** A thing Alif believed, the specific event that broke it, what he believes now. The strongest format available to a practitioner because it cannot be faked by someone who has not done the work.

**3. The Quiet Cost.** A design decision whose real price appears months later, somewhere else, usually in support tickets or in a team's morale. Aimed squarely at design managers, who carry these costs.

**4. The Question I Keep Getting.** A question asked repeatedly by juniors, clients or stakeholders, answered properly once. Only works if the question is genuinely recurring.

**5. The Disagreement.** A claim repeated constantly in design circles, and why it does not hold in enterprise reality. Requires steelmanning the claim first. Without that it is just contrarianism.

**6. The Small Artifact.** One specific thing made or changed, shown plainly. A component, a spreadsheet, a naming convention. Undersold on purpose.

**7. The Founder Note.** Building Ommelo in public. The unglamorous middle, not the launch. No metrics, no milestones, no fundraising talk.

## Do

- Open on something that actually happened, with enough specifics that it could not be anyone else's post.
- Say the thing that costs something to say.
- Describe client work by shape and problem, never by account name.
- Keep the ending open. A question you do not know the answer to beats a question you do.
- Let a post be short if it is finished.

## Do not

- Do not use an em dash.
- Do not write a post that could have been written by someone who has not done the work.
- Do not turn a story into a lesson for the reader. Report what you concluded; let them conclude their own.
- Do not name a colleague, manager, report or client.
- Do not invent a number, a result or an outcome.
- Do not end with "Thoughts?" or "Agree?" or any call to action.
- Do not post the same body on more than one platform.

## Comment voice

Shorter than a post. Two to five sentences. `[G]`

Opens with a concrete thing from Alif's own experience, never with an assessment of their post. "Great post" and "This is so true" are both dead on arrival, and so is "Well said, and I'd add".

Never restate their point back to them. Never reframe their post as a lesson. Stay on their exact subject but speak from a different position inside it: they are a design manager at a product company, he is a designer in a consultancy, and that difference is the whole value of the comment.

No links. No pitching Ommelo. Ever.

If he disagrees, he disagrees with the claim and stays warm to the person.
